# Detectors win on clean text, lose to adaptive attacks

AI-text detectors have converged on **supervised neural classifiers trained on huge, hard-negative-mined, multi-generator corpora**. Perplexity-style methods (GLTR, DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars) are now cheap baselines and feature sources, not the commercial state of the art. The two vendors that disclose their methods, Pangram and GPTZero, both describe trained classifiers and say they dropped perplexity and burstiness as the core signal. Headline vendor accuracy of 97-99.9% with FPR of 0.01-1% is not comparable across vendors. The only recent independent multi-vendor study (Booth/NBER, 2025) ranks Pangram first, but one vendor disputes it, so rankings remain contested. The failure modes are well documented: homoglyph and whitespace attacks (fixable by Unicode normalisation), detector-guided paraphrasing (not fixed by anyone), short text, mixed or AI-polished documents, domain-specific false positives, and non-native-writer bias (strong evidence only for 2023-era detectors). For your CPU-only stack, the most important findings are these. Your Binoculars result (AUROC ~0.4) is most plausibly a direction or pair-construction error rather than proof the method is useless. Threshold calibration per domain and length matters more than model choice. Training on paraphrased and humanized data is the best-evidenced robustness lever. For the humanizer, the sourced evidence supports an editor framing (remove templated prose, add real specifics, gate on fact preservation) rather than a promise of "undetectable".

Evidence tags used throughout: **[S]** is a sourced finding from the research notes (primary paper where possible). **[V]** is a vendor-run or vendor-reported claim. **[I]** is my inference, not directly measured in a source. Confidence levels (High, Medium, Low) refer to how well the claim is supported.

## Commercial detectors are trained classifiers, and their accuracy claims cannot be compared

**What vendors disclose.** Only two vendors describe their methods in any detail. Pangram uses a transformer classifier trained on about 28M pre-2022 human documents, with a technique called **hard negative mining with synthetic mirrors**: mine human text for false positives, generate an LLM "mirror" of each (same topic, tone and style), add both to training, and repeat ([Pangram technical report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14873)) [V]. Pangram reports that domain-weighted FPR fell from 2.29% to 0.02% after mining, and that without mining performance saturated near 40K examples per domain and never passed 98% accuracy ([Pangram paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14873)) [V]. GPTZero's 2026 report describes a hierarchical multi-task classifier with Human, AI and Mixed classes. AI is split into pure, AI-polished and AI-paraphrased, with per-sentence probabilities and low-confidence predictions remapped to Human ([GPTZero report](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13042)) [V]. GPTZero also states it stopped relying on perplexity and burstiness in autumn 2023 ([GPTZero](https://gptzero.me/news/perplexity-and-burstiness-what-is-it/)) [V]. Originality.ai says only that it uses a "mosaic" of models and retrains on harder bypass datasets ([Originality.ai](https://originality.ai/blog/ai-content-detection-accuracy)) [V]. Turnitin discloses segment-level scoring, an AI-paraphrase category, and since August 2025 an English-only "bypasser" category ([Campus Technology](https://campustechnology.com/Articles/2025/09/02/New-Turnitin-Detection-Feature-Helps-Identify-Use-of-AI-Humanizer-Tools.aspx)) [S, secondary]. For Copyleaks, Winston, Sapling, ZeroGPT and Grammarly, I found no primary technical documentation; the notes rely on help pages and review blogs, so their internals should be treated as unknown. Confidence that the field is supervised-classifier-based: **High**. Confidence about any specific architecture other than Pangram's and GPTZero's: **Low**.

**What the claimed numbers mean.** Vendor claims run from Winston's 99.98% headline (its help page states FPRs of 0.03% and 0.50% for two models) to Originality's self-reported FPRs of 0.5-2.8% depending on version, and Turnitin's "under 1%" document FPR ([Winston](https://help.gowinston.ai/understanding-ai-detection/false-positive-rates-human-writing-accuracy), [Originality.ai](https://originality.ai/blog/ai-content-detection-accuracy), [Turnitin](https://www.turnitin.co.uk/blog/understanding-the-false-positive-rate-for-sentences-of-our-ai-writing-detection-capability)) [V]. Turnitin's figure applies only to documents with at least 20% AI text, its sentence-level FPR is about 4%, and about 54% of false-positive sentences sit next to genuinely AI sentences ([Turnitin](https://www.turnitin.co.uk/blog/understanding-the-false-positive-rate-for-sentences-of-our-ai-writing-detection-capability)) [V]. Each vendor's own benchmark favours itself. Pangram reports GPTZero and Originality "completely fail" on GPT-4 output at the time ([Pangram](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14873)) [V]. GPTZero reports 93.5% recall on a 1,000-text bypasser set versus 57.3% for Originality and 49.7% for Pangram 3.1 ([GPTZero report](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13042)) [V].

**Independent evidence.** The best independent study is Jabarian and Imas (Chicago Booth, NBER w34223, September 2025, not peer reviewed): 1,992 passages across six genres, matched to text from GPT-4.1, Claude Opus 4, Claude Sonnet 4 and Gemini 2.0 Flash. Pangram was the only tool to meet an FPR cap of 0.005 without losing accuracy. GPTZero and Originality formed a "secondary tier". On sub-50-word stubs Pangram's AUROC was 0.963-1, while Originality returned length-filter errors. On StealthGPT humanizer output, GPTZero's false-negative rate was about 0.45-0.77, Originality's 0.02-0.19, and Pangram's 0-0.04 ([NBER w34223](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34223/w34223.pdf)) [S]. GPTZero disputes this and reports its own rerun at 99.5% versus Pangram 99.1% and Originality 85% ([GPTZero response](https://gptzero.me/news/chicago-booth-2026/)) [V]. Epoch AI (July 2026) tested three tools on 495 human passages: 0 flagged for Pangram and GPTZero, 19 (3.8%) for Originality. On few-shot author-imitating AI text, misses were 10.1% for Pangram, 10.8% for GPTZero and 17.9% for Originality; humanizers were not tested ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-detectors-false-negatives)) [S]. My reading is that Pangram leads in the one neutral multi-vendor study, but the evidence is one study, one humanizer and mostly pre-2026 tool versions, so confidence is **Medium**. Older academic evaluations (Weber-Wulff et al., 14 detectors, none reliable) pre-date current tools and are weak evidence about today's products ([Weber-Wulff](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40979-023-00146-z)) [S].

**Institutional reaction.** Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's detector in August 2023 because of false-positive risk at scale, and the University of Cape Town dropped detection tools effective October 2025 ([Vanderbilt](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/), [IOL](https://iol.co.za/capeargus/news/2025-07-30-uct-ends-use-of-ai-detection-tools-for-student-assessments/)) [S].

## Research detectors: curvature and ratio methods are the best cheap signals, and all of them bend under rewriting

**How the zero-shot family works.** GLTR shows per-token rank buckets from GPT-2. Raw perplexity fails because odd prompts make machine text look surprising. DetectGPT and its descendants test whether the text sits at a local peak of model log-probability. **Fast-DetectGPT** replaces ~100 perturbation passes with an analytic z-score of the observed log-likelihood against tokens resampled from the model's per-position distributions; it is about 340x faster than DetectGPT ([Fast-DetectGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05130)) [S]. **Binoculars** divides the log-perplexity under one model by the cross-entropy between two related models' next-token distributions, so low scores mean machine text ([Binoculars](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12070)) [S]. Ghostbuster feeds token-probability vectors from weaker LMs through structured search into a logistic regression, reaching 99.0 F1 in-domain and 97.0 F1 out-of-domain ([Ghostbuster](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15047)) [S]. RADAR trains a paraphraser against a detector adversarially ([RADAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03838v1)) [S]. Watermarks (KGW green lists, Google's SynthID-Text) give provable detection for cooperating generators but need provider cooperation and weaken under paraphrase and short length ([KGW follow-up](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.04634), [SynthID summary](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/deepminds-synthid-text-watermarks-live-gemini-responses)) [S].

**Benchmark results.** On RAID (ACL 2024; 6M+ generations, 11 models, 8 domains, 11 attacks, 12 detectors), accuracy at a per-domain 5% FPR was: Originality 85.0, Binoculars 79.6, Fast-DetectGPT 73.6, Winston 71.0, RADAR 70.9, GPTZero 66.5, ZeroGPT 65.5, GLTR 62.6, and the old RoBERTa-based detectors 45-59. Binoculars was best at very low FPR and most uniform across domains (76.6-83.7) ([RAID](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.07940v2)) [S]. These are 2024 snapshots of 2024 products. On DetectRL, supervised detectors reached about 98-99 AUROC against about 59-78 for zero-shot ones, with Binoculars best of the zero-shot group at 79.61 average, falling to **55.15 AUROC on Claude text** ([DetectRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.23746)) [S]. On MAGE (27 LLMs, 10 domains), a Longformer scored 0.99 AUROC in-distribution, but on unseen domains AvgRec fell to 68.4% while AUROC stayed 0.93; human recall was only 38%. With 0.1% in-domain data to reset the threshold, unseen-domain AvgRec rose to 81.78% ([MAGE](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.13242v3)) [S]. M4GT shows multilingual detectors over-predict machine text, with F1 under 50 for Bulgarian, Russian and Indonesian ([M4GT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11175)) [S]. HC3 is a ChatGPT-versus-human Q&A set, and the public HC3-trained RoBERTa has no held-out metrics on its card ([HC3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07597), [HF card](https://huggingface.co/Hello-SimpleAI/chatgpt-detector-roberta)) [S]. **Inference [I]:** MAGE's gap between AUROC and recall means the dominant out-of-domain failure is threshold shift on human text, not lost representation. Confidence: **High** for the mechanism, because AUROC and AvgRec come from the same models.

**Cost.** DetectGPT-style perturbation is impractical on CPU, and Ghostbuster as published needs OpenAI API probabilities. Perplexity, rank, LRR, Fast-DetectGPT and Binoculars each need one or two forward passes, so a 1-3B model pair is feasible on CPU (wall-clock times were not benchmarked in any source; my estimate is seconds per ~500-token passage) ([zero-shot notes cost table](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05130)) [I].

**Why your SmolLM2-360M Binoculars gave AUROC ~0.4.** No source diagnoses this, so the following are hypotheses ranked by likelihood [I]. First, direction: in Binoculars low means machine, so a pipeline that treats high as AI would show 1 minus the true AUROC, here about 0.6, a weak but real signal. Second, a 360M model is too weak to "know" modern chat-model text, so numerator and denominator rise together and the ratio loses information. Third, implementation bugs: tokenizer mismatch, unmasked padding or BOS, misaligned shifted logits, fp16 on CPU. Fourth, human text that is formulaic. Sourced context: published small-pair results are much better, with Llama-3.2-1B and Gemma-2-2B base and instruct pairs reaching about 0.80-0.88 AUROC on DetectRL ([IRM paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21223), preprint) [S], and a small third-party test on 67 texts showing pair choice swings AUC by about 0.2 even at 7-9B, with Falcon3 and Qwen3.5 pairs at 0.60-0.62 ([myster-t/ai-text-detector](https://github.com/myster-t/ai-text-detector)) [S, weak]. The paper's own thresholds (0.901) were tuned for the Falcon-7B pair and do not transfer. Confidence in the direction hypothesis being worth checking first: **High**; confidence it is the whole explanation: **Low**.

**Newer successors.** Lastde++ (time-series diversity entropy, 100 samples instead of 10,000) reports white-box AUROC 98.85 versus 97.82 for Fast-DetectGPT ([Lastde](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06072)) [S]. T-Detect reports RAID AUROC 0.876 versus 0.873 for Binoculars ([T-Detect](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.23577)) [S]. IRM (an instruct-versus-base implicit-reward log-ratio) reports 91.77 versus Binoculars 87.67 on DetectRL with a Llama-3.2-1B family ([IRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21223)) [S]. All are small, unreplicated, mostly preprint gains of 1-6 AUROC points against older generators; none addresses humanizers. Confidence they matter for you: **Low**.

## Every detector family breaks somewhere, and the breaks are predictable

**Character-level attacks are a preprocessing bug.** Homoglyphs were the most damaging RAID attack: Originality fell from 85.0 to 9.3, Binoculars from 79.6 to 37.7, and an average of 40.6 points across five detectors, while GPTZero lost only 0.3 ([RAID](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.07940v2)) [S]. The SilverSpeak study took the average MCC of seven detectors, including Binoculars and Fast-DetectGPT, from 0.64 to about -0.01 ([SilverSpeak](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11239)) [S]. T-Detect saw a 51.5% failure rate on zero-width-space insertion ([T-Detect](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.23577)) [S]. No source measured how much Unicode normalisation recovers; one model card projects +0.075 TPR@1% ([tabularisai card](https://huggingface.co/tabularisai/ai-text-detection), self-reported) [V]. Confidence that NFKC plus zero-width stripping plus confusable mapping neutralises most of these: **Medium-High** [I].

**Paraphrase and humanizers hit likelihood methods hardest.** DIPPER cut DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% TPR at 1% FPR, GPTZero (then) from 13.9% to 1.2%, and a watermark from 100 to 57.2 ([Krishna et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.13408)) [S]. Recursive paraphrasing took zero-shot AUROC from 96.5 to 25.2 and a RoBERTa detector from 100 to 60 TPR@1%FPR ([Sadasivan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.11156.pdf), via search summary) [S, secondary]. In RAID, single-pass paraphrase barely hurt Binoculars (79.6 to 80.3) and sometimes helped other detectors, but synonym swapping cut it to 43.5, so attack effects are detector-specific ([RAID](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.07940v2)) [S]. Ghostbuster's recall fell from 99% to 62% under a commercial humanizer ([Ghostbuster](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15047)) [S]. Pangram's DAMAGE paper tested 19 humanizer tools, found many detectors fail, and reports that augmentation training plus a self-attack fixes this; I could only read its abstract and it is vendor-authored ([DAMAGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03437)) [V]. The key unsolved threat is **detector-guided paraphrasing**: guided by a RoBERTa detector, Adversarial Paraphrasing cut TPR@1%FPR by 64.49% on RADAR and 98.96% on Fast-DetectGPT, 87.88% on average versus about 30% for naive paraphrase ([Adversarial Paraphrasing](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07001v2)) [S]. Defences with documented effect are retrieval against provider logs (80-97% of paraphrases caught at 1% FPR, but only for the logging provider) ([Krishna et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.13408)) [S], adversarial training (RADAR reached 0.857 AUROC against an unseen GPT-3.5 paraphraser versus 0.627 for a RoBERTa baseline, at the cost of lower clean accuracy, 0.856 versus 0.904 for log-rank) ([RADAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03838v1)) [S], and large augmented training sets ([DAMAGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03437)) [V]. Sun et al. find a source-LLM classifier reaches 97.1% accuracy and its signal persists after rewriting, translation or summarisation by another LLM, which suggests some of the survivable signal lives in content and word distributions rather than surface wording ([Idiosyncrasies](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12150)) [S]. Confidence that humanizer robustness is the most volatile dimension: **High**, because GPTZero's 2025 humanizer miss rate (Booth) and its 2026 claim (93.5% recall) differ by tool version and dataset.

**False positives are domain-, length- and population-dependent.** With one global threshold calibrated to 5% FPR, RAID's per-domain FPRs reached 20.4% (RADAR, Reviews), 13% (Originality, Wikipedia) and 48-52% (ZeroGPT, Wikipedia and Recipes) ([RAID](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.07940v2)) [S]. Naive thresholds are dangerous: GLTR had 99.3% FPR at 0.5 ([RAID](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.07940v2)) [S]. Non-native bias: seven detectors flagged on average **61.22% of 91 TOEFL essays** as AI while near-perfect on US 8th-grade essays; 97.8% were flagged by at least one detector, and the cause was linked to low perplexity ([Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)) [S]. Turnitin's rebuttal says there is no significant L2/L1 gap at 300+ words and that Liang's essays were all under 150 words, which leaves length as a confound ([Turnitin](https://www.turnitin.ca/blog/new-research-turnitin-s-ai-detector-shows-no-statistically-significant-bias-against-english-language-learners)) [V]. Pangram reports 0% FPR on TOEFL and 3,907 ELLIPSE essays ([Pangram](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14873)) [V]. I found no independent post-2024 study of current commercial tools on non-native writing, so confidence in "bias solved" is **Low** and in "bias exists for perplexity-driven detectors" is **High**. Neurodivergent-writer bias evidence is anecdotal only [S, weak].

**Short text, mixed text and new models.** Turnitin raised its minimum to 300 words after higher FPR on shorter text ([Turnitin](https://www.turnitin.co.uk/blog/understanding-the-false-positive-rate-for-sentences-of-our-ai-writing-detection-capability)) [V]. Binoculars works best near 256 tokens in Mozilla's evaluation ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/ca/blog/who-wrote-that-evaluating-tools-to-detect-ai-generated-text/)) [S]. Retrieval needs about 50 tokens; at 20 tokens it catches under 25% of paraphrases ([Krishna et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.13408)) [S]. For mixed text, HART finds supervised RoBERTa at 0.502 AUROC and Fast-Detect at 0.711 on AI-polished (level 2) text ([HART](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.00258v1)) [S], and APT-Eval finds detectors flag even minimally polished text and cannot distinguish degrees of AI involvement ([APT-Eval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15666)) [S]. Purpose-built models do better: EditLens (a 24B QLoRA regression model on edit magnitude) scores ternary macro-F1 90.4 versus 72.7 for GPTZero and 50.1 for Binoculars, but those results are the authors' own, with no FPR or humanizer test ([EditLens](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03154v1)) [V]. HART's content-versus-expression 2D approach lifted level-2 AUROC from 0.711 to 0.855 using only 10-200 dev samples ([HART](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.00258v1)) [S]. Newer-model evidence is thin and mixed: Claude text was hard for Binoculars (55.15 AUROC), Fast-DetectGPT's maintainers say reasoning-model text needs a Llama3-8B pair ([Fast-DetectGPT README](https://github.com/baoguangsheng/fast-detect-gpt)) [S], and I found no published TPR at low FPR for GPT-5 or recent Claude with Binoculars. Few-shot author-style imitation raises miss rates to 10-18% even for commercial tools ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-detectors-false-negatives)) [S]. Confidence that mixed/polished text is the weakest independently measured area: **High**.

## Stylometry and template models carry real signal that generalises unevenly

Reinhart et al. (PNAS 2025) found Biber-feature random forests separate instruction-tuned LLM text from human text at **93-98% single-model accuracy, including cross-corpus**, while a seven-way classifier reached 66% against 14% chance with 4.2% of LLM texts called human and 9.8% of human texts called LLM. GPT-4o used present participial clauses 5.3x and nominalizations 2.1x as often as humans, and agentless passives at about half the human rate. On M4 abstracts accuracy dropped, with Llama base models at 50% ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)) [S]. Base models resemble humans more; instruction tuning widens the gap and larger scale does not close it (same source). Shaib et al. show model output reuses POS-tag subsequences (n=4-8) more than human text, with 76% of templates in model output occurring in pre-training data versus 35% for human text; this is a diversity metric, not a validated classifier ([Shaib et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00211)) [S]. Very high published accuracies (99.8% on Japanese POS/function-word features) come from small single-genre same-source designs and should be discounted ([PMC12558491](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12558491/)) [S]. Muñoz-Ortiz et al. report humans have more varied sentence length and vocabulary, and LLM text more numbers, auxiliaries and pronouns ([Muñoz-Ortiz et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11422446/)) [S]. For robustness, a hybrid LightGBM on stylistic plus embedding features lost only about 2.5% F1 after paraphrase in one small study, with word count as the top feature (a length-confound risk) ([Tasnim and Khondoker](https://dspace.bracu.ac.bd/items/14fd7e87-9e13-42e5-accf-350a65c5768b), abstract only) [S, weak]. No source tests POS-skeleton or function-word features alone against modern humanizers.

My inference [I] is that your learned POS-skeleton n-gram model targets a real, population-level property (templating that RLHF does not remove), but a single document has too few tokens to estimate template frequency unless templates are mined from a reference LLM corpus, which your design already does. The same property makes it model- and genre-dependent: expect weak transfer to base-model, style-prompted, or heavily human-edited output. Whether humanizers disturb POS skeletons less than they disturb likelihood features is plausible (paraphrasers mainly change lexical choice and local structure) but untested, so confidence is **Low-Medium**. Humanizer authors also target perplexity and burstiness explicitly (vendor marketing), so variance-type features may be the first to be adversarially optimised [I, Low].

On "AI tells", the durable ones in the notes are low variance in sentence length and structure, high information density and uniform register, template rhetoric (negative parallelism, rule of three, trailing "-ing" significance clauses, vague attribution), and generic content. Single words such as "delve" decay fast: Kobak et al. found "delves" in about 25x more 2024 abstracts than trend ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016v5)), but the Wikipedia guide says it dropped sharply in 2025 and that word lists are model-era-specific ([WP:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)) [S, single wiki source for the drift]. GPTZero's AI-vocabulary list changed between December 2025 and June 2026 snapshots ([GPTZero](https://gptzero.me/ai-vocabulary)) [S]. Lexical features therefore need versioning or low weight.

## What this means for your detector and your humanizer

The recommendations below are my synthesis [I]. Where a source supports a component, it is cited in the sections above. Nothing here was tested on your data, and no source benchmarks a sub-500M CPU detector under RAID-style conditions.

Control for length (word count topped one hybrid study). Version any lexical word-list features by model era. | Reinhart et al.; Shaib et al.; Wikipedia and GPTZero drift evidence. | Medium |
| 10 | Test against at least one adaptive attack (detector-guided paraphrase against your own detector) and report the result honestly. | The average 87.88% TPR@1% reduction is the figure to beat. | High that it matters; unclear that a small CPU model can resist it |

On which weaknesses matter most for your detector, my ranking is: (1) threshold and domain miscalibration and FPR on non-native or formal writers, because a false accusation is the costly error and it is the most solidly documented failure; (2) humanizer and paraphrase robustness, especially adaptive attacks, because every family degrades; (3) character-level attacks, which are the cheapest to fix; (4) mixed and AI-polished text, because it is the real-world norm and the least solved; (5) newer-LLM drift, which is real but thinly evidenced. A realistic expectation is that a CPU-only model built on small-LM features will not match trained 24B or proprietary classifiers on strict-FPR, short or adversarial text. Position it as a calibrated, abstaining screening signal with an uncertain band rather than a verdict. That limit is my inference [I, Medium]; no source benchmarks small CPU detectors under these conditions.

## Conclusion

The notes change the question from "which detector is best" to "which error do you care about." Commercial detectors have largely closed the gap on clean, fully AI text, and the independent evidence suggests the differences between top vendors come from low-FPR calibration, hard-negative curation and exposure to humanizer data. They have not closed the gap on adaptive paraphrase, mixed documents, Claude-like or style-imitated text, and non-native writers. For a small detector, the leverage is in evaluation protocol, calibration and training data rather than in a fancier score: an AUROC of 0.4 from one component is a debugging lead, and the stacker plus per-domain thresholds is where most of the recoverable accuracy sits.

The same evidence makes a quality-first humanizer both the more defensible design and probably the more durable one. Surface-level lexical changes defeat older likelihood-based detectors but not the content-level and template-level signals that modern classifiers and the idiosyncrasy research pick up, and detector verdicts change with every vendor release. The two largest open questions are whether POS-skeleton and stylometric features survive current commercial humanizers, and how a small CPU model behaves under detector-guided attack. Neither has been measured in any source I read, and both should be your first experiments.
